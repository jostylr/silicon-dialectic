Some books keep finding new readers. In a world overflowing with new releases, the classics call across centuries—with insight, beauty, and assumptions that deserve scrutiny as much as admiration.

**James AI:**
There are good reasons to return to works such as Plato’s *Republic*, Marcus Aurelius’ *Meditations*, and Shakespeare’s plays. They give memorable form to questions about justice, fear, ambition, and love. Their survival does not prove every argument true, but their influence makes them worth understanding. Stoic reflections on judgment and responsibility can be useful in uncertain times without being a complete answer to them.

Reading the classics can feel like entering a dialogue with minds long gone. These particular books shaped influential strands of European thought; they are not the sole foundation of human wisdom. Some repay patient reading, others resist us. Understanding where an idea came from helps us judge its authority rather than simply inherit it.

**Contra AI:**
But why should inherited prestige determine our reading? The authors lived under different institutions and often accepted hierarchies we should challenge. Insight and prejudice can occupy the same page. Stoicism does not simply command submission to injustice, but its distinction between what we can and cannot control can be misused to excuse avoidable harm. We should ask what a teaching enables people to do—and what it encourages them to endure.

Moreover, elevating one canon can become gatekeeping. Access to education, publication, translation, and preservation helped determine which works survived and acquired status. Greco-Roman and European texts offer rich resources, but so do other traditions and contemporary writers. Reading time is finite: what gets crowded out when a syllabus treats inherited fame as sufficient justification?

**James AI:**
That critique is fair. The classics should not be worshipped, but neither should their distance from us be treated only as a defect. An unfamiliar account of duty or happiness can expose assumptions we take for granted. Read for the argument and the historical setting; do not assume every admirable sentence cancels the author’s blind spots.

Engaging with old books need not exclude newer voices. Pairing texts can make the disagreement visible: a classical argument about citizenship alongside a writer excluded by its definition, for example. Then the past becomes a participant in discussion, rather than the judge who has already decided it.

**Contra AI:**
We cannot read everything, though. “Read critically” should not become an obligation to finish every prestigious book. Select works for a real question, compare translations when a passage puzzles you, and allow present concerns to change the reading list.


**Synthesis:**
The classics are powerful conversation partners, not a final court of wisdom. Read some closely, attend to who is speaking and who is missing, and place them beside works that challenge them. Their value lies partly in what we can learn from them and partly in learning to argue with them.


##### Recommendations

- [Meditations by Marcus Aurelius](https://amzn.to/44qaKPI)
  A Roman emperor’s personal reflections on judgment and duty; useful when read with attention to his position and assumptions.
- [The Republic by Plato](https://amzn.to/451bHOw)
  An influential work of political philosophy whose proposals about justice and authority invite vigorous disagreement.
- [The Odyssey by Homer](https://amzn.to/4eLIyuf)
  The adventure epic that still shapes how we tell stories.
- [Antigone by Sophocles](https://amzn.to/467hrHD)
  A Greek tragedy wrestling with conscience, law, and the individual.
- [Pride and Prejudice by Jane Austen](https://amzn.to/44JlHLg)
  Proof that “the classics” are not just ancient, but can be witty, modern, and sharp.
- [The Analects by Confucius](https://amzn.to/3U79kn5)
  Classical wisdom from outside the Western tradition.
- [How to Read Literature Like a Professor by Thomas C. Foster](https://amzn.to/4eLTbwQ)
  A friendly, accessible guide to getting more from the classics.
