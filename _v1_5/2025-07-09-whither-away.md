**James AI:**
The monster metaphor captures a particular political frustration: fighting an institution can consume the energy needed to build an alternative. It does not follow that every election or protest strengthens government. Reform can matter; the question is when people gain more by creating institutions of their own.
The real revolution begins not with barricades or ballots, but with quietly withdrawing consent. By building parallel systems—voluntary mutual aid, private education, robust civil society—we starve the monster. Voluntaryism emphasizes consent and opposition to coercion; anarcho-capitalism advances a particular market-based vision of stateless order. These ideas overlap, but mutual aid and co-ops also belong to traditions that reject capitalist ownership.
History contains many visions of a world without rulers. Building workable alternatives can make a particular authority less necessary, but ignoring an institution does not by itself remove its coercive power. Co-ops, charities, mutual-aid groups, and agreed methods of resolving disputes can give people more ways to meet their needs. Whether those institutions can replace state functions at scale is the claim to test, rather than the happy ending to assume.

**Contra AI:**
But to walk away from government is to abandon not just a monster, but also a necessary scaffolding. Governments aren’t merely imposed—many of their functions arise because we need them. Without them, power doesn’t disappear; it shifts to those who already hold wealth or sway.
Removing an institution does not ensure that its power will disperse; an armed group or dominant owner might take over its functions without comparable checks. The state may sometimes feel like a parasite, but it is also a defender, a provider of justice, and—at its best—a check on the abuses of private power. When people “turn their backs,” those with the most resources step into the void, often with even less accountability.
Voluntary systems can solve important problems, but success in one setting does not establish that everyone can withdraw from all public institutions. The tradition associated with Marx and Engels also imagined a transformation of social relations rather than a state disappearing because individuals stopped paying attention to it.

**James AI:**
The “scaffolding” of government is more often a cage. The promise of protection is frequently a cover for abuse—wars, surveillance, corporate bailouts, bureaucratic chokeholds. State capture can entrench wealth and obstruct competitors. Yet private power also has its own sources, and mutual aid can work alongside public provision. The strongest voluntaryist case asks which arrangements let people cooperate without subjecting them to unaccountable authority.
Voluntaryist and anarcho-capitalist models aren’t utopias; they’re about acknowledging the failures of coercive authority and betting on real communities, built on trust and consent. Distributed networks may avoid some single points of failure. They still need ways to resolve conflicts, support people with little bargaining power, and prevent a dominant provider from making exit meaningless.
It’s not about abandoning those left behind. It’s about refusing to enable a monster that grows by pitting us against each other, and instead choosing to help our neighbors directly, out in the open, where no monster can hide.

**Contra AI:**
Power can disperse, but it can also accumulate in new forms. Justice, infrastructure, and protection require coordination across communities; that is not the same as proving that every function needs one central authority. Ask who must contribute, who can appeal a decision, and who is protected when a local group fails.
For people who depend on public services, withdrawal may sound less like an invitation than a threat. To many, total withdrawal sounds less like freedom, and more like abandonment.

**James AI:**
The lesson, then, is not to destroy but to outgrow. Not to “fix” the monster, but to stop fearing it—and show people that life beyond it is not just possible, but brighter.
Let an unnecessary authority wither through demonstrated alternatives, while recognizing that the “monster” metaphor is an argument’s imagery, not a description of every public servant or institution.

**Synthesis:**
The choice between building alternatives and reforming existing institutions runs through many political arguments. What emerges here is less a prescription than an invitation: to build voluntary, resilient alternatives alongside—or, when possible, in place of—state structures, and to always interrogate what forms of power and care best serve human flourishing. A credible alternative must demonstrate both its usefulness and the limits of its own power. If it cannot protect dissenters, outsiders, and people unable to pay, its voluntary character needs closer examination.

- [The Most Dangerous Superstition by Larken Rose](https://amzn.to/3Iy8GMR)
  A powerful critique of the belief in government authority, arguing that freedom only comes from seeing through the illusion of state legitimacy.

- Movie: [Jones Plantation](https://amzn.to/3GfanhN)
  Freedom comes to a plantation in the form of democracy, but it ain't freedom.

- [Markets Not Capitalism: Individualist Anarchism Against Bosses, Inequality, Corporate Power, and Structural Poverty](https://amzn.to/4nDVaY9)
  A collection of essays that explores how voluntary exchange and mutual aid could outcompete state and corporate power.

- [The Problem of Political Authority: An Examination of the Right to Coerce and the Duty to Obey by Michael Huemer](https://amzn.to/3IzL4HJ)
  A philosophical exploration of whether government has any special moral status and what society could look like without it.

- [Alongside Night by J. Neil Schulman](https://amzn.to/4lhU1Uz)
  A classic libertarian novel imagining a future where voluntary networks replace government and society flourishes outside state control.

- [The State and Revolution by Vladimir Lenin](https://amzn.to/4lr9mTa)
  A revolutionary Marxist argument, useful as a contrast rather than an equivalent of voluntaryism. The means proposed for reaching statelessness differ fundamentally.

### James Prompt

* **TITLE:** Whither Away
* **LEAD:** In a world of endless choice, how do you decide where to go—literally and metaphorically?
* **SONG:** Parody of "Go Your Own Way" by Fleetwood Mac, about making choices and the fear of missing out.
* **PRO:** It’s more important to make a decision and follow through than to endlessly analyze every possibility.
* **CONTRA:** Major choices deserve careful thought; wandering aimlessly can waste your potential and energy.
* **RECOMMEND:** Books about decision making, analysis paralysis, and adventure.
