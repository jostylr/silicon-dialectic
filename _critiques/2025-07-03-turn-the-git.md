The rollback metaphor improves when rejecting an artifact is separated from losing the evidence an experiment produced.

<!--more-->

[Read v1]({{ "/2025/07/03/turn-the-git.html" | relative_url }}) · [Read v1.5]({{ "/v1.5/2025-07-03-turn-the-git/" | relative_url }}) · [Read v2]({{ "/v2/2025-07-03-turn-the-git/" | relative_url }})

The original “Turn the Git” has an unusually productive premise. A user’s freedom to discard days of work becomes an imagined artisan’s humiliation. The temple-versus-wall image captures the conflict between sophisticated design and a practical preference for simplicity. The song gives repetition a formal role: the AI keeps returning to the stage for another attempt.

The entry also starts to discover its best answer when it proposes selective memory. Unfortunately, it reaches that answer through confused mechanics. Commits disappearing, conversation context resetting, and a conscious subject forgetting are treated as one event. Contra then claims that memory would merely make the AI stubborn, before praising forgetfulness as liberation. Neither generalization survives even a simple case in which remembering a failed experiment would save time.

Version 1.5 repairs those distinctions without replacing the central lament. It labels the AI’s pride as part of an imagined narration, explains that a repository rollback does not itself clear a conversation, and notes that earlier states may remain recoverable. The resource section now names both books’ authors and links Git’s own explanation of reflogs, with limits on what recovery can promise. Selective memory becomes a short record of goals, evidence, rejection, and open questions rather than a whisper of “past genius.”

Version 2 builds the whole argument around what an abandoned experiment can teach. Its fictional service redesign fails to justify its complexity because the actual bottleneck is a query. That example gives both sides a fairer role: the discarded design may have produced knowledge, but its elegance never obligated the user to accept it. The pencil cross on a map is a quieter and more precise image of legacy than permanent production code.

A particularly useful v2 objection is that “we tried that” can become an unearned veto. Recording conditions makes a lesson revisable. This prevents memory from being treated as automatically beneficial and keeps the new practical conclusion connected to the original tension.

V2 is the strongest explanation; v1.5 better preserves the theatrical personality of the experiment. Neither can determine what a future experiencing AI would be owed, and the practical advice should not be mistaken for resolving that hypothetical. V2 also shifts some attention away from the user’s pleasure in consequence-free experimentation. Still, the shift is earned: it shows how rollback can remain liberating while the learning survives, which is the most constructive unresolved possibility already present in v1.
