Authorship becomes more persuasive when the essay tests responsibility instead of awarding meaning for intention alone.

<!--more-->

[Read v1]({{ "/2025/07/02/impostor-syndrome.html" | relative_url }}) · [Read v1.5]({{ "/v1.5/2025-07-02-impostor-syndrome/" | relative_url }}) · [Read v2]({{ "/v2/2025-07-02-impostor-syndrome/" | relative_url }})

Version 1 stages a sharp anxiety: the returning programmer has a project in their name but an assistant doing much of the visible work. Its satire, especially the escalating exclusion signs and unsettling lullaby, carries a fear of being tolerated only as a spectator. The opening is more memorable than a conventional discussion of automation and employment would have been.

The dialogue, however, gives both speakers shortcuts. Contra announces that these are no longer the human’s projects and treats future replacement as inevitable. James replies that care and intention preserve authorship. The final sentence—meaning measured “only by intent”—is too generous. A person can intend well while approving work they do not understand, and another can contribute substantially without being uniquely capable of doing the task.

Version 1.5 preserves the satirical escalation but changes what the debate must prove. It qualifies claims about AI consistency, replaces passive-review accusations with a specific competence concern, and removes the forecast that direction will soon be unnecessary. Its revised synthesis asks whether the human can explain design choices, verify behavior, and revise the result. These are more demanding grounds for authorship than simply caring. Corrections in the reading list also remove the loose description of Marcus du Sautoy as a philosopher and temper the sweeping claim that technological innovation has always been spiritual.

Version 2 moves from imagined exclusion to a recognizable review failure: accepting an elaborate framework because its explanation sounds professional. That example makes room for an uncomfortable concession. Some feelings called impostor syndrome reveal a real gap between responsibility and understanding. The essay does not confuse acknowledging that gap with proving that a person does not belong.

Its strongest additional distinction is between responsible contribution and the lost pleasure of craft. Someone may be a competent assisted developer and still prefer to make certain things manually. That preference needs no claim that manual work is morally superior. The new song’s visitor badge and hidden beams keep the anxiety close to the project rather than projecting an entire dystopia.

V2 is the stronger argument, but the original is the sharper satire. Both revisions risk making accountable review sound easier than it is: explaining a design is not the same as catching every consequential fault. V2’s prediction-and-check exercise supplies a beginning, not a complete method. The honest verdict is that authorship can survive delegation, while the amount of independent understanding required remains dependent on the work and the people affected by failure.
