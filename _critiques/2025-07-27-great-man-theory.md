Credit becomes more informative when both the hero story and the inevitability story accept the burden of historical specificity.

<!--more-->

[Read v1]({{ '/2025/07/27/great-man-theory.html' | relative_url }}) · [Read v1.5]({{ '/v1.5/2025-07-27-great-man-theory/' | relative_url }}) · [Read v2]({{ '/v2/2025-07-27-great-man-theory/' | relative_url }})

V1 knows why stories of genius are appealing. Einstein supplies a recognizable person through whom a reader can approach conceptual change, and the celebratory song gives that admiration a playful form. The opposing voice also correctly pushes toward collective contribution. The central problem is that both positions rely on counterfactual confidence. One side asks who else could possibly have done it; the other assures us that someone would have filled the gap. Neither can establish its imagined history merely by stating it.

The treatment of chronology is more concrete and more serious. The original groups Lorentz, Poincaré, Minkowski, and Hilbert together as if their contributions all occupy the same stage in the emergence of relativity. The prompt specifically suggests Minkowski already had relativity in hand. Einstein's 1905 special-relativity paper and Minkowski's 1908 spacetime formulation must be distinguished. General relativity and Hilbert belong to a later story. A defense of collective work should not need to rearrange that sequence.

V1.5 makes the chronological correction directly while retaining the prompt unchanged as a record of the experiment. It also replaces certainty about an Einstein-free world with acknowledged uncertainty, distinguishes persuasive reasoning from force of personality, and asks the collective account to identify actual contributions. The retained lyric's “spacetime inventor” is flagged as poetic shorthand rather than silently presented as history. The truncated Sleepwalkers title is repaired and its author supplied.

V2 starts with the impossibility of obtaining a photograph of the alternate history. It builds a fresh argument around a crowded notebook and distinguishes priority, development, communication, and influence. This makes room for individual excellence without treating an inventor or founder as the author of every supporting technology. Its practical extension—supporting the conditions of discovery while leaving room for unusual individual judgment—is more useful than the first version's symphony metaphor.

Yet the new essay discusses a method of writing history more than it demonstrates that method. It supplies one important chronological correction, then relies on generalized possibilities involving archives, tools, and recognition. A detailed account of one Einstein paper's development would give the argument firmer historical texture. The essay also cautiously extends from scientific discovery to business without exploring how commercial control changes the distribution of credit.

For this entry, v1.5 is the best match to the original blog's ambition: a compact, corrected exchange that retains the song and admiration. V2 is the stronger framework for thinking about historical credit, but less a biography-inflected blog post than a methodological essay. Its broader scope is valuable at a cost. The unresolved question is not whether individuals or communities matter; it is how to assess their particular contributions without converting uncertainty into either worship or inevitability.
