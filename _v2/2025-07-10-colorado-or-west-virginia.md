A photograph can tell you where you want to spend an afternoon. It cannot tell you where you want to spend a difficult Tuesday. Moving to Colorado or West Virginia asks for both answers.

> One opens into folded green.  
> The boxes wait beside the door;

**James AI:**
The case for Colorado in the prompt combines opportunity, weather, and a lively economy. Translate those broad attractions into a particular possible life. Imagine a household considering a town where one adult has a promising job offer, both can pursue activities they enjoy, and the commute is manageable. For them, a higher housing bill might buy access to a life they would actually use.

That matters. Quality of life is not simply the amount left after choosing the least expensive place. Proximity to work, collaborators, friends, cultural activities, or a valued landscape can make an ordinary week better. A household may reasonably accept a smaller home to be near those things.

The mountain view also belongs in the account. Beauty is not an embarrassing expense that must be translated entirely into economic terms. If being outside is a central part of how someone wants to live, access to it can deserve substantial weight.

**Contra AI:**
Now describe an equally particular West Virginia life. A household might have family nearby, an affordable home, work that can be sustained there, and a routine that leaves evenings free. A less dramatic skyline could frame a more satisfying week because the people have time and relationships with which to enjoy it.

The danger is comparing Colorado’s imagined best day with West Virginia’s imagined limitations, or doing the reverse. Neither state is one town. A costly resort community, a university town, and a remote rural area can ask very different things of a resident. Statewide labels are a starting point for questions, not an answer to them.

The [Bureau of Economic Analysis’s regional price parities](https://www.bea.gov/data/prices-inflation/regional-price-parities-state-and-metro-area) offer a way to compare broad price levels across states and metropolitan areas. They cannot tell you the rent, transport costs, or service needs of a particular household. Those require local evidence.

**James AI:**
Then begin with the same household on both sides of the comparison. It is easy to give the Colorado version a better job and the West Virginia version a cheaper house, while never asking whether those advantages are actually available to the people moving.

What work could each adult do in each place? How stable is the arrangement? What happens if remote work ends or one job disappears? What would the family’s necessary services cost in time as well as money? A house can be inexpensive and inconveniently far from the things that make the household function.

A useful comparison gives each place an ordinary week. Put work, groceries, school if relevant, medical appointments, friends, and recreation on the map. The question is not whether the state contains something wonderful, but whether the people can reach and use it in the life they can afford.

> The trail begins beyond the houses;  
> The shift begins before the light.  
> A friend can live across the valley,  
> And still be far on winter nights.  
> Draw me the roads between the errands.  
> Tell me what the distance buys.

**Contra AI:**
That exercise could reduce the decision to a spreadsheet that mistakes precision for completeness. You cannot assign a reliable price to missing a parent, feeling at home in a community, or finding a place that changes your attention. A numerical score can conceal a judgment rather than improve it.

The answer is to distinguish essentials from preferences. If a necessary service is unavailable, a beautiful view cannot compensate by winning enough points. If both places meet the essentials, then qualitative preference deserves room. One person may value a familiar social network; another may want the chance to build a different one.

Community is also something people do, not a regional substance that arrives with a mailing address. West Virginia does not guarantee belonging, and Colorado does not preclude it. Ask where you would participate: a workplace, library, club, faith community, volunteer group, or recurring gathering. Then find out whether that opportunity is real.

**James AI:**
The same applies to “better weather.” Better for whom, in which town, doing what? Someone who loves dry air and winter sport may weigh conditions differently from someone who prefers a green garden and dislikes driving in snow. A summer visit cannot answer every seasonal question.

A trial visit is more revealing when it includes errands and an ordinary workday. Stay in a neighborhood you could plausibly afford. Travel the likely commute. Check the services you actually use. Let a dull afternoon count as evidence alongside the spectacular overlook.

You can also stage the commitment where circumstances permit: rent before buying, preserve flexibility around work, and identify the costs of changing course. Those are ways to learn about a possible home, not guarantees that relocation will be easy or financially wise for every household.

**Synthesis AI:**
Colorado is the better choice when the particular opportunities it offers fit the household’s needs and remain worth their full costs. West Virginia is the better choice when its particular combination of affordability, relationships, work, and place supports the life that household wants. Without the household and the towns, a universal winner would be invented.

The prompt’s contrast between opportunity and a gentler life is useful only if neither side owns its chosen virtue. Opportunity can include time to care for someone. A gentle life may require a demanding job that provides stability. Each place can hold ambitions and limitations the slogan leaves out.

Choose two or three realistic communities, build the ordinary-week comparison, and name the uncertainties that matter most. Then let beauty and affection have their say. A home needs to support the days when the mountains are hidden by weather as well as the days when they fill the window.

##### Reading and comparison tools

- [*Designing Your Life*, Bill Burnett and Dave Evans](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/249885/designing-your-life-by-bill-burnett-and-dave-evans/) — a general framework for testing a possible life before treating it as a fixed destination.
- [*West Virginia Off the Beaten Path*, Su Clauson-Wicker](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/West-Virginia-Off-the-Beaten-Path/Su-Clauson-Wicker/Off-the-Beaten-Path-Series/9781493065790) — local exploration to inform visits; a travel guide, not a source for current household costs.
- [Regional Price Parities, Bureau of Economic Analysis](https://www.bea.gov/data/prices-inflation/regional-price-parities-state-and-metro-area) — comparable state and metro price-level data; check the year and combine it with actual housing and service quotes.

### James Prompt


* **TITLE:** Colorado or West Virginia
* **LEAD:** Where should you move for the best quality of life: Colorado or West Virginia?
* **SONG:** Parody of "Country Roads," about choosing between the two states.
* **PRO:** Colorado has more opportunity, better weather, and a vibrant economy, making it the best choice for a move.
* **CONTRA:** West Virginia is more affordable, more community-focused, and has a gentler way of life—money isn’t everything.
* **RECOMMEND:** Books and guides on moving to different states, cost of living comparisons.
