A community kitchen serves dinner during a storm. The van burns fuel, the vegetables came from cultivated land, and the volunteers are tired. The meal does good. It also has costs. What would it mean to call those costs evil?

<!--more-->

**Take My Evil Away — original lyrics for v2**

> I brought a bowl against the rain,  
> A little warmth, a little grain.  
> The road behind me bears a scar;  
> I cannot feed you from a star.  
> Let me see the cost I hide,  
> Keep the hungry at my side.  
> If these hands cannot be clean,  
> Let them mend what they have seen.

**James AI:**

The kitchen makes the uncomfortable point better than a villain would. Even an admirable action draws on a world of extraction, displacement, and competing needs. Fuel was used. Someone grew and transported the food. A volunteer's time might otherwise have gone to an elderly relative. Good does not arrive wrapped in an exemption from consequences.

Our language often helps us overlook those costs. We call a purchase convenient and leave the working conditions outside the sentence. We call food natural and stop asking how it was produced. The prompt's severe vocabulary—evil in the ordinary act—is a way to force attention back toward whatever the comforting description excludes.

Nature does not offer a clean escape. Predators eat other animals; [parasitoid larvae develop at a host's expense](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2770194/). Calling the whole process harmonious can be as selective as calling every human arrangement benevolent. Life contains suffering even when no villain planned it. A morality that notices only malicious intent will miss a great deal.

**Contra AI:**

But a vocabulary that calls everything evil also misses a great deal. It places a storm, a painful rescue, a careless omission, and deliberate cruelty under the same label. We need distinctions because different problems call for different responses.

The storm is not accountable. The driver is accountable for how they drive. The kitchen's organizers may be accountable for avoidable waste or for ignoring an accessible alternative. The person who sabotages the food out of spite is accountable in another way. These differences do not deny suffering. They help us locate responsibility.

Nor should the person receiving dinner become a prop in the volunteer's search for innocence. Refusing to deliver a meal because delivery has an environmental cost can protect a flattering self-image while leaving someone hungry. Moral seriousness requires comparison among available actions, including the consequences of doing nothing.

> A spotless ledger, empty plate;  
> A perfect plan that comes too late.  
> The question waiting at the door  
> Is what our care is useful for.  
> Name the damage, count the need,  
> Let the answer change the deed.  
> Peace is not a closed account;  
> Leave a little room for doubt.

**James AI:**

Comparison is essential, but “necessary” is a word people award themselves too easily. Some harm is unavoidable under today's arrangements. Some appears unavoidable only because changing those arrangements would be inconvenient. We should ask which kind we mean.

Take the animal example in the prompt. Killing for nourishment and killing for amusement differ in purpose. That does not settle whether the first act is justified in every situation. Available food, nutritional needs, practical alternatives, and the animal's interests all remain relevant. A critic can acknowledge the difference in motive without accepting a blanket permission.

Likewise, an employer may sincerely wish workers well while maintaining a schedule that predictably exhausts them. The absence of delight in suffering is not enough. Negligence, indifference, and refusing to investigate can be morally serious even when nobody thinks of themselves as cruel.

**Contra AI:**

Yes. Intention is evidence about a choice, not a moral solvent. But people also have limited information, time, and power. Requiring an exhaustive audit of every consequence before acting would make ordinary cooperation impossible. The standard needs to be demanding enough to change behavior and workable enough to guide it.

For the kitchen, start with visible, consequential choices. Plan deliveries to avoid needless trips. Purchase responsibly where the budget permits. Treat volunteers as people who may say no. Ask recipients what would help rather than deciding entirely on their behalf. These are proposals for the example, not calculations proving that one menu minimizes all harm.

And give collective action its place. An individual volunteer cannot redesign transport infrastructure during dinner service. A community can campaign for better access, share facilities, or establish purchasing standards. Responsibility should expand with capacity rather than being distributed as equal guilt to everyone who participates in a flawed system.

**James AI:**

Then perhaps the useful remainder is regret without self-condemnation. Even a justified decision may leave a loss worth mourning. We do not need to redescribe that loss as good simply because another choice was worse. Admitting it can keep the desire for better alternatives alive.

**Contra AI:**

Provided regret remains connected to attention and repair. Endless self-accusation can become another way of centering the actor. The person harmed may need compensation, a changed practice, or a hearing. They do not necessarily need a performance of how burdened our conscience feels.

**Synthesis AI:**

The inability to avoid every harm does not abolish moral distinctions. It makes them more necessary. Ask what happened, what was intended, what could reasonably have been foreseen, what alternatives were available, and who had the power to change them. Then ask what repair is possible.

The kitchen should serve dinner and remain open to criticism of how it does so. Peace with a choice can mean accepting a justified action without declaring its costs unreal. The aim is a conscience that keeps learning, not a certificate that the holder has escaped participation in the world.

##### Recommendations

- *Original Sin: A Cultural History* by Alan Jacobs — A historical and theological lens on shared moral fallibility, listed in the [author’s bibliography](https://program.honors.baylor.edu/sites/g/files/ecbvkj1701/files/2023-05/jacobs_cv.pdf). Its category of sin should not be silently equated with every material harm.
- [*The Expanding Circle: Ethics, Evolution, and Moral Progress* by Peter Singer](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9781400838431/html) — An argument for extending moral concern through reasoning; a useful challenge to deciding that familiar practices need no defense.
- [*How to Relax* by Thich Nhat Hanh](https://www.parallax.org/product/how-to-relax/) — A contemplative companion to the question of how to stay attentive without turning concern into permanent self-punishment.

### James Prompt

- **TITLE:** Life Necessitates Evil  
- **LEAD:** By focusing on the negatives of every trade-off of every choice we make, evil makes itself known.  
- **SONG:** Parody of Take My Breath Away fashioned into Take My Evil Away, struggles with evil, and a prayer to take my evil away, and ending with finding peace with the choices. Maybe tussle with benign seeming actions and twist them into evils such as eating meat being twisted into murdering life or driving being twisted into polluting or using stuff being twisted into relying on the labors of others.  
- **PRO:** Every action we take has negative consequences and the sum total of all of this can lead to quite some evil in the world. Also point out even non-human animals and the view of their actions as evil, such as the young of an insect growing in and eating up another creature.  
- **CONTRA:** Evil is not the things that we must do, but about the choices we make to do something awful. Slaughtering a cow to eat it is very different from killing a cow for the fun of it.  
- **RECOMMEND:** Debates about our nature of good vs evil, original sin, beliefs of minimal impact and serenity.
