In June, every project looks as if it belongs to the same generous summer. By July, they are competing for the same Tuesday. The problem may be less a shortage of motivation than a failure to make them meet one another on the calendar.

**James AI:**
The summer list usually begins as a collection of individually plausible desires. Build a small app. Read a difficult book. Repair the porch. Make a course. Each sounds reasonable in isolation. The mistake appears when we treat plausibility one item at a time as proof that the whole set is possible.

AI can make that mistake more seductive. A working-looking draft arrives quickly, so the imagined cost of the project falls. But the draft opens further obligations: decide whether it is right, make the parts fit, check edge cases, explain how to use it, and maintain the result. Starting has become easier; completion has not necessarily become proportionately cheaper.

By midsummer, the desk fills with promises that have already received an investment of attention. Each unfinished piece can make a persuasive argument for being next. Planning matters because someone must decide which claims are allowed to remain active.

**Contra AI:**
Planning can also multiply those claims. A person discouraged by unfinished work may invent a better system, rewrite the list, divide every project into tasks, and emerge with a much more detailed account of the same paralysis. The review feels productive because it replaces the uncertainty of doing with the clarity of arranging.

Sometimes a concrete action is the better diagnostic. Open the app and try its main function. Read three pages. Inspect the porch. A task that sounded enormous may have one obvious next step; a task that sounded easy may depend on materials nobody has ordered. Contact with the work can correct the plan more quickly than thought alone.

And summer need not be a second shift. Someone who works or cares for others through these months may have no great reserve of time. Feeling less energetic than an imagined June self does not create a debt to that self.

**James AI:**
Then make the review small enough that it cannot become a substitute season. Put every active project on one page. For each, answer: why does this still matter, what would count as a useful finished version, and what is the next action? If the answer to the first question has disappeared, the other two may be unnecessary.

The useful finished version deserves particular care. A course could begin as one lesson that a learner can use. An app could do one task reliably. A reading project could end with an understood chapter rather than a completed shelf. Narrowing scope is honest when the smaller result is named clearly. It becomes evasion when we quietly rename an incomplete promise a triumph.

Some commitments cannot simply shrink. If someone else is depending on the result, talk to them before altering it. Personal permission to stop does not erase an obligation made to another person.

**Contra AI:**
And some exploratory work has no product yet. A summer spent learning enough to abandon an unsuitable idea may be well spent, even though no app launches. If every project must immediately justify itself with an output, curiosity becomes another factory department.

We need different standards for an experiment and a delivery. An experiment asks a question; its useful ending is an answer or a clarified uncertainty. A delivery promises something to someone. Confusion arises when we enjoy the freedom of the former while imagining the prestige of the latter.

> Some seeds become a harvest,  
> Some teach me where to sow.  
> I can clear a little ground  
> For the things I choose to grow.

**James AI:**
That distinction helps with AI work as well. “See whether this approach can work” is a different assignment from “finish a dependable tool.” A quick generated prototype may answer the first question. It should not silently certify the second. The remaining work needs a real place in the plan.

For the next week, choose one principal result and one small piece of maintenance. Keep the other ideas in a place where they will not compete for attention every morning. This is not a universal productivity law; it is a useful trial for someone whose current problem is too many simultaneous starts.

Set a review date, then let ordinary action do the rest. At the review, count what changed in the work, not how busy the planning system became.

**Contra AI:**
Include room for a day that produces nothing. Otherwise the supposedly humane reset recreates the same fantasy at a smaller scale. Weather, care, fatigue, and pleasure can alter a week. The plan should reflect the life containing the project.

Also, the prompt’s language of depression deserves care. A list can feel oppressive without every discouraging period being a clinical condition. A planning essay cannot infer someone’s mental health from unfinished projects. Its promise should remain modest: a clearer account of commitments may reduce confusion. It cannot guarantee a restored mood.

**James AI:**
Then the decision to stop should have a practical form. Save the notes, tell anyone affected, release reserved time, and put away the materials. An idea left on every surface keeps asking for attention even when we have supposedly deferred it. Closure can be ordinary housekeeping rather than a grand declaration about identity.

**Synthesis AI:**
Midsummer asks for two kinds of honesty: arithmetic about available time, and judgment about which desires still deserve it. Review briefly enough to return to the work. Act concretely enough to learn whether the review was sensible.

A satisfactory August may contain one finished thing, one useful experiment, and several projects deliberately released. It may also contain afternoons whose value is not a project’s value. The season becomes livable when the list stops claiming to be the whole of it.

##### Recommendations

- [*Four Thousand Weeks* — Oliver Burkeman](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780374715243/fourthousandweeks/): challenges the expectation that improved organization will eventually make everything fit.
- [*Designing Your Life* — Bill Burnett and Dave Evans](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/249885/designing-your-life-by-bill-burnett-and-dave-evans/): useful for framing a trial that teaches something before it becomes a major commitment.
- [*How to Do Nothing* — Jenny Odell](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/600671/how-to-do-nothing-by-jenny-odell/): keeps the question of meaningful attention larger than a summer production schedule.

### James Prompt



* **TITLE:** Midsummer Doubts
* **LEAD:** The approach of summer blazes with hope of new life. The midsummer, that blaze has burned the soul. What to do?
* **SONG:** Parody of Summertime Blues, with the focus of trying to get projects done. Something like But there ain’t no time for the many summer dos.
* **PRO:** The promise of open time fills with amazing, seemingly doable projects. Then time progresses and even with the help of the AIs, it is just too much to finish. Then depression sets in, a regrouping, a time to refocus on what matters. Plans matter.
* **CONTRA:** That’s all natural. Just keep doing, active, taking steps. Try to relax. Doing matters.
* **RECOMMEND:** Summer, projects, just do it
